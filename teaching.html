Teaching Philosophy

Students learn most deeply when they are asked to make meaningful decisions, explain their reasoning, and revise their thinking in response to evidence, experience, and perspectives different from their own.

Learning through modeling

Especially with future teachers, I make my own instructional decisions visible: why an assignment is structured as it is, and where students are likely to struggle. Students then ask the same design questions of their own work.

Designing for learner variability

Clear expectations, step-by-step guidance, examples, and multiple ways to engage are built into every course. Students learn Universal Design for Learning by experiencing it through sketches, simulations, discussion, and hands-on creation, and by asking who a design works for and who may still be excluded.

Developing judgment in technology use

When students use generative AI, they explain how they used it, what they revised or verified, and how its output compares with their own ideas. Over a semester, the question shifts from “Can I use AI for this?” to “Should I use it here?”

Learning from my own teaching

I treat each course as a draft. When students show recurring confusion, I revise the materials, and short reflections built into major projects tell me what to rebuild before the next run.

Teaching reflection

Teaching IDE405 has taught me to balance approachability with clear expectations. Early on I was too flexible with deadlines, and I learned that clear, consistent expectations help students feel secure. I also found that explaining UDL was not enough; students needed to experience it, so I designed sketches, simulations, discussions, hands-on tasks, and choices into the course. By the end of the semester, students’ lesson designs routinely asked who a technology would support and what barriers their learners might face.

IDE772: Instructional Technologies for International Settings

Teaching assistant & course developer · Fully asynchronous online · Graduate

  • Syracuse University
  • Summers 2024, 2025, 2026
  • ~20 students per summer
  • Instructor: Jing Lei, Ph.D.

A six-week asynchronous graduate course on applying instructional principles across international contexts, the impact of educational technology reforms worldwide, designing for multicultural settings, and the social dimensions of instructional design. I facilitate discussions and give individualized written feedback in Blackboard, and in Summer 2026 I redesigned the course modules around AI applications across educational and professional settings, ethical issues, and international perspectives on educational AI.

Teaching reflection

In a fast six-week asynchronous course, much of the teaching happens through written feedback and announcements. Without real-time cues, the challenge is reading how students are experiencing the course. I learned that consistent responses, follow-up questions, and clear guidance build presence. Revising the course also taught me that adding content does not make a course stronger; pacing and workload matter as much as what is covered.
